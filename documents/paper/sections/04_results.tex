\section{Results}

This section presents the experimental results of the study, organized according to the two analytical scopes defined in Section~I: (1) the classification performance comparison between ConvNeXt-Tiny and DeiT-Small, and (2) the faithfulness comparison between Grad-CAM and Attention Rollout as measured by deletion and insertion AUC.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Selection}

Both ConvNeXt-Tiny and DeiT-Small were fine-tuned using a two-phase training scheme (linear-probe followed by full fine-tuning) under a grid search over learning rate and number of training epochs.
For each architecture, the configuration achieving the highest validation macro-F1 was selected as the final model, following the model selection protocol described in Section~III-A.

Both architectures reached an identical test accuracy of $87.99\%$, each correctly classifying $557$ of the $633$ test images, so accuracy alone cannot separate them.
Based on the test-set macro-F1, DeiT-Small (87.74\%) marginally outperformed ConvNeXt-Tiny (87.63\%) -- a gap of only $0.11$ percentage points, small enough that the two architectures are effectively comparable in overall discriminative power.
Since the macro-F1 values were not exactly tied, the diabetic retinopathy recall tie-breaker was not formally invoked; however, it is worth noting that ConvNeXt-Tiny attained a substantially higher DR recall ($99.39\%$ vs. $95.76\%$) and macro ROC-AUC ($98.15\%$ vs. $97.06\%$), a discrepancy discussed further in Section~IV-E.
Per the strict macro-F1 ranking, DeiT-Small is treated as the best-performing architecture; both architectures' checkpoints are nonetheless carried forward into the explainability analysis in the following subsections, since faithfulness is compared architecture-to-architecture (Grad-CAM vs. Attention Rollout) rather than reduced to a single winner.

The per-class results in Table~\ref{tab:per_class_performance} show where the two models differ despite their equal accuracy.
The two models share the same cataract recall ($91.67\%$), while DeiT-Small is stronger on the normal class ($88.82\%$ vs. $85.71\%$ recall) and ConvNeXt-Tiny is stronger on diabetic retinopathy ($99.39\%$ vs. $95.76\%$ recall).
Both models show the same weak point: glaucoma has the lowest recall of the four classes ($74.17\%$ for ConvNeXt-Tiny and $74.83\%$ for DeiT-Small), and it is also the smallest class in the test set ($151$ images).

Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion_matrix} shows the confusion matrices of both models on the test set, in raw image counts.
ConvNeXt-Tiny missed only $1$ of the $165$ diabetic retinopathy images, compared with $7$ for DeiT-Small.
For glaucoma, the errors of both models follow the same pattern: the misclassified images are assigned mostly to normal ($18$ for ConvNeXt-Tiny, $19$ for DeiT-Small) and to diabetic retinopathy ($14$ for both), and only rarely to cataract ($7$ and $5$).
In the opposite direction, normal images are most often mistaken for glaucoma ($15$ and $12$), indicating that the glaucoma--normal boundary is the main source of error for both architectures.

\subsection{Qualitative Explainability Results}

Fig.~\ref{fig:xai_qualitative} presents qualitative examples of Grad-CAM heatmaps generated from ConvNeXt-Tiny and Attention Rollout heatmaps generated from DeiT-Small on representative test images from each class.
For glaucoma and diabetic retinopathy, both methods concentrate their highest activations tightly on the optic disc region -- the clinically relevant structure for assessing cupping in glaucoma and a common site of hemorrhages and exudates in diabetic retinopathy -- indicating that both models learned to attend to anatomically meaningful evidence rather than spurious background cues for these two classes.
For the normal example, both methods likewise center on the optic disc and surrounding vasculature, consistent with this being the reference structure clinicians inspect first.
Cataract is the weakest case for both methods: Grad-CAM activates a narrow vertical strip near the image border rather than a diffuse haze pattern (the actual visual signature of a cataract in a fundus photo), while Attention Rollout spreads its attention toward the image corners and edges outside the fundus circle.
This is consistent with cataract's presentation as a global loss of image sharpness/contrast rather than a spatially localized lesion, which is inherently harder for patch-level saliency methods -- both CNN- and attention-based -- to localize.
Attention Rollout's tendency to assign non-trivial weight to border/corner patches across multiple classes also matches its characterization in Section~III-B as producing more diffuse heatmaps than Grad-CAM.

\subsection{Faithfulness Evaluation}

To quantitatively assess the faithfulness of the generated explanations, deletion and insertion AUC were computed for Grad-CAM on ConvNeXt-Tiny and Attention Rollout on DeiT-Small using patch-based perturbation, as described in Section~III-C.
The scores were computed on $40$ randomly sampled test images per architecture, each explained with respect to the class predicted by the model.

Both metrics are read from a curve of the model's confidence in its predicted class as the image is perturbed patch by patch in the order given by the heatmap.
For deletion, the curve starts at the confidence on the original image and the most important patches are removed first, so a faithful heatmap makes the confidence fall quickly and yields a small area under the curve.
For insertion, the curve starts at the confidence on the blurred baseline and the most important patches are restored first, so a faithful heatmap makes the confidence rise quickly and yields a large area.
Because both the confidence and the fraction of perturbed patches lie in $[0, 1]$, each AUC also lies in $[0, 1]$ and can be read as the average confidence retained over the whole perturbation sequence.
Table~\ref{tab:faithfulness} summarizes the resulting scores, averaged over the sampled images.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\caption{Deletion and Insertion AUC of Grad-CAM and Attention Rollout ($n=40$ sampled test images)}
\label{tab:faithfulness}
\centering
\begin{tabular*}{\columnwidth}{@{\extracolsep{\fill}}lcc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{XAI Method} & \textbf{Deletion AUC} $\downarrow$ & \textbf{Insertion AUC} $\uparrow$ \\
\midrule
Grad-CAM (ConvNeXt-Tiny) & \textbf{0.3906} & 0.6982 \\
Attention Rollout (DeiT-Small) & 0.4626 & \textbf{0.7263} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular*}
\end{table}

A deletion AUC of $0.3906$ therefore means that ConvNeXt-Tiny retains on average about $39\%$ confidence in its prediction while the patches ranked highest by Grad-CAM are progressively removed, against about $46\%$ for DeiT-Small under Attention Rollout.
Conversely, an insertion AUC of $0.7263$ means that DeiT-Small holds on average about $73\%$ confidence while the patches ranked highest by Attention Rollout are progressively restored, against about $70\%$ for ConvNeXt-Tiny under Grad-CAM.
The two methods thus split the faithfulness comparison: Grad-CAM is more faithful under deletion (a margin of $0.072$), while Attention Rollout is more faithful under insertion (a smaller margin of $0.028$).
This is consistent with the qualitative pattern in Fig.~\ref{fig:xai_qualitative}: Grad-CAM's sharper, more concentrated activations identify a small set of patches whose removal quickly lowers the confidence, whereas Attention Rollout's broader coverage is less precise but recovers the confidence slightly faster as patches are restored.
Since each method is paired with a different architecture, these scores compare the two model--explanation pairs rather than the two explanation methods in isolation.

Table~\ref{tab:faithfulness_per_class} breaks the scores down by predicted class.
Both methods obtain their lowest deletion AUC on glaucoma ($0.136$ for Grad-CAM and $0.089$ for Attention Rollout), where the pathology is concentrated at a single, well-localized structure (the optic disc), so removing the few highlighted patches is enough to collapse the prediction.
Cataract shows the opposite behavior, with the highest deletion AUC for both methods ($0.687$ and $0.845$): the confidence stays high even after the top-ranked patches are removed, matching the qualitative finding that neither method localizes cataract's diffuse presentation well.
A likely contributing factor is the perturbation baseline itself: a heavily blurred fundus image resembles the hazy appearance of a cataract, so replacing patches with blur removes little cataract evidence.
The same effect would explain why Attention Rollout reaches a high insertion AUC on cataract ($0.860$) while both methods obtain their lowest insertion AUC on the normal class ($0.527$ and $0.445$), since a blurred starting image already favors the cataract prediction and many sharp patches must be restored before the model becomes confident that an image is normal.
The per-class values are each averaged over only $8$ to $12$ images and should be read as indicative.

\subsection{Discussion}

Taken together, the classification and explainability results indicate that overall predictive accuracy and explanation faithfulness are not the same axis of comparison, and neither architecture dominates on both.
DeiT-Small edges out ConvNeXt-Tiny on test macro-F1 by a negligible margin ($87.74\%$ vs. $87.63\%$), while ConvNeXt-Tiny is clearly preferable on diabetic retinopathy recall ($99.39\%$ vs. $95.76\%$) and macro ROC-AUC ($98.15\%$ vs. $97.06\%$) -- and its paired explanation method, Grad-CAM, is also the more faithful of the two under deletion.
For a clinical screening system where missing a diabetic retinopathy case is the costlier error and clinicians need tightly localized visual evidence to trust a prediction, ConvNeXt-Tiny with Grad-CAM is the more defensible choice despite its very slightly lower macro-F1.
Conversely, DeiT-Small with Attention Rollout attains marginally higher overall macro-F1 and a higher insertion AUC, which may suit contexts that value broader contextual corroboration over pinpoint localization.
This reinforces the motivation stated in Section~I: accuracy comparison alone is insufficient for selecting a deployable architecture, and faithfulness metrics surface trade-offs that accuracy hides.

This study has several limitations.
The hyperparameter search covered only $3$ learning rates $\times$ $5$ epoch budgets ($15$ configurations) per architecture, so the reported results reflect the best configuration found within this grid rather than a globally optimal one.
Faithfulness scores were computed on a $40$-image subsample of the test set per architecture rather than the full test set, for computational tractability; the resulting deletion/insertion AUC values should be read as estimates.
The qualitative comparison in Fig.~\ref{fig:xai_qualitative} is illustrative (one image per class) rather than an exhaustive audit, and a larger-scale clinical validation with ophthalmologist-annotated lesion masks would be needed to more rigorously confirm the anatomical relevance of the highlighted regions.
Finally, both models were fine-tuned and evaluated on a single dataset of $4{,}217$ images from one source; generalization to other fundus imaging devices, populations, or class distributions remains untested.
